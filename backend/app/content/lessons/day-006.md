## You are called in as the advisor

The date is October 2013. You are an outside advisor, and a senior official forwards you a message from the launch room of a very public government project: the Healthcare.gov marketplace for health insurance. The site went live this morning.

```chat
# Launch room, day one (reconstructed for teaching, not verbatim)
Operations | Launch room | People are reaching the sign-up page, but many cannot finish creating an account.
Delivery lead | Contractor team | Traffic is far higher than we planned for. We are working through defects.
Official | Programme office | The date was fixed for political reasons. We could not move it. What do we do now?
```

The message above is a teaching reconstruction, not a transcript. The facts below are summarised from widely reported accounts.

```note
Treat figures and details as approximate and verify them against the primary government reviews before you quote them anywhere. This lesson teaches the pattern, not the exact numbers.
```

## What was reported

```cards
alert | Struggled from day one | Many users could not create accounts or complete enrolment, and traffic far exceeded what the system handled. | A launch that looks like a failure on the first day is hard to recover trust from.
users | Split ownership | Delivery was spread across many contractors, with a government agency acting as its own integrator. Reviews pointed to unclear ownership and weak coordination. | Nobody owns the whole, so everyone owns a part.
clock | Late change, late testing | Requirements changed late and the testing window was compressed. End-to-end testing of the whole system came very late. | Defects found by the public instead of by testers.
flag | An immovable date | A fixed political launch date was treated as immovable, while scope and readiness were not. | Quality becomes the thing that flexes by default.
rocket | The recovery | A small group of senior engineers set daily priorities and tracked defects visibly. The site improved substantially within weeks. | Recovery needed the clarity that planning never had.
```

## How a failure like this builds up

Read the sequence as a timeline of decisions, not a timeline of bugs.

```timeline
Before build | Launch date locked and treated as immovable | warn
During build | Scope and requirements keep changing | warn
Late in build | Testing window compressed, end-to-end testing very late | bad
Launch | Public failure, many cannot enrol | bad
After | Small senior group, daily priorities, visible defect tracking | ok
```

```flow
Fixed date | Launch date locked early
Late changes | Scope moves, testing squeezed
Unclear ownership | Nobody owns the whole
Launch | Public failure
Recovery | Visible defects, daily priorities
```

No single mistake caused it. A date that could not move, plus changes that kept coming, plus no one clearly accountable for the whole, made failure very likely before launch day.

```ref
PMI | PMBOK Guide, stakeholder engagement and project governance | Failed launches often trace to weak governance and engagement, not to code. Use that framing when asked what you would check first on a troubled project.
```


## Your decision as advisor

```decision
? One week before launch the testing is incomplete and the date is fixed. What do you advise?
- Launch on the date and fix problems live, since the date cannot move | The public finds the defects. Trust is damaged and recovery is far harder than a short delay would have been.
- Quietly extend internal testing and say nothing to leadership | Nobody with authority knows the real risk, and the decision is made without evidence.
+ Put a written go or no-go standard in front of the decision maker now, with the open defects and risks stated plainly, and recommend a limited launch if the date truly cannot move | The decision maker has evidence and options. Even if the date holds, the risk is chosen openly instead of discovered by the public.
= When a date cannot move, protect the decision. Give leaders facts, a clear standard and a smaller blast radius, instead of silent heroics.
```

## Symptoms versus root causes

The skill this lesson trains is separating what users saw from why it happened.

```quiz
? Which of these is a root cause rather than a symptom?
- Users could not sign up on day one
+ A fixed launch date with no clear authority to call a no-go
- The site was slow
= Symptoms are what users see. Root causes are the decisions and structures that made failure likely.
```

```ref
ITIL | ITIL 4 problem management practice | Separate the incident (symptom) from the problem (root cause). Say this in any lessons-learned or recovery interview question.
```


## The same pattern, today

The story is from 2013, but the pattern repeats in AI projects now. Imagine Daniel Okafor, sponsor of Lighthouse, receiving this message:

```quote
A competitor launched an AI assistant. We need ours live before the board meeting. | Executive sponsor
```

```cards
flag | A date that cannot move | The launch date is set before anyone knows how accurate the assistant is. | Quality is judged only after customers see it.
users | Nobody owns the whole | Data, model, app and support sit with different teams. | Each team passes the blame when it fails.
clock | Evaluation squeezed | Impressive demos are easy; careful testing of wrong answers takes time. | The first real users find the failures.
```

```ai
Shift | AI projects repeat classic failures faster, because a demo is easy to build and proper evaluation is hard.
Example (illustrative) | A public launch date is fixed, and the demo looks strong. The PM asks for a written go or no go standard covering load, end to end testing and rollback before the date is confirmed.
Use AI to | Summarise public review reports, extract recurring causes into a table and draft questions for a go or no go review.
Keep human | The conclusions. Public reports can be partial, so check the current record before you quote a detail.
Risk | A launch driven by a fixed date and a convincing demo, with no tested limits and no one allowed to say not ready.
Control | Agree a written go or no go standard before the date is fixed, including accuracy on a test set, load testing and a rollback plan.
Interview line | The failure pattern is old: fixed dates, weak end to end testing and unclear ownership. For AI I add a measured accuracy standard and a named person who can stop the launch.
```

```cards
network | Integration owner | One person accountable for how all parts work together. | Many teams build parts and nobody owns the whole.
cpu | AI engineer | Builds the model features and tests them with real volumes. | Demo quality mistaken for launch readiness.
shield | AI security consultant | Reviews exposure and abuse before public launch. | Public launch before review.
handshake | Forward deployed engineer | Sees real user behaviour first and reports it. | Reports ignored because the date is fixed.
wrench | Platform engineer | Handles capacity and release controls. | No load test at expected peak.
```

```incident
# Public AI assistant launched on a fixed date (illustrative)
What happened | A public sector AI assistant launched on a politically fixed date after only partial testing of how its parts worked together.
Impact | Users met errors at peak times and trust in the service fell.
Root cause | No one owned end to end readiness, load and integration testing were incomplete, and the date was treated as a constraint rather than a decision.
Resolution | The launch was scaled back to a limited group, an integration owner was named, testing was completed and the wider launch followed once criteria were met.
PM move | Set go live criteria in advance, name one owner for the whole, and give leaders a clear choice between date and readiness.
```

## Questions to think about

```accordion
What were the root causes, not just the symptoms? | Symptoms are crashes and queues. Root causes sit in governance, ownership, scope control and when testing happened.
Which PM practices were weak or missing? | Look at integration management, scope control, testing, risk escalation and the go or no-go decision.
Who should have been able to say "not ready"? | Ask who had both the authority and the evidence, and why the structure let a launch go ahead anyway.
What did the recovery team do that you can reuse? | A small empowered group, daily priorities, visible defect tracking, clear decisions.
```

```answer
? What can project managers learn from a failed high profile launch like Healthcare.gov?
- The technology was bad, so choose better technology | Oversimplifies. Public reviews point to delivery and governance issues as well.
- Nothing, it was a one off | Similar patterns recur in large launches, including AI ones.
+ I focus on patterns that apply to any high visibility launch: a single owner for the whole system, integrated and load testing before go live, go live criteria agreed in advance, and honest status reporting that does not hide red items. For the specific public details I would check the current record rather than rely on memory. I apply the same checks to AI launches.
= Never quote figures you cannot verify. Say you would check the record.
= Use the case to show patterns, not to blame people.
= Link to a current project: where could the same pattern appear?
```

```mindmap
# Why launches fail
Ownership | No single owner of the whole, unclear decisions
Testing | Integration, load, end to end
Governance | Fixed dates, hidden red status, late bad news
Learning | Go live criteria, staged launch, review after
```

```flashcards
Symptom versus root cause | A symptom is what users see. A root cause is the delivery or governance gap behind it.
Go live criteria | Agreed conditions that must be met before launch, set in advance.
Staged launch | Release to a limited group first so problems are small and reversible.
Why one owner of the whole | Parts can pass alone and still fail together.
```
