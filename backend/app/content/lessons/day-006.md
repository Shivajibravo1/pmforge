## A failure everyone could see

In October 2013 the United States government launched Healthcare.gov, the online marketplace for health insurance under the Affordable Care Act. On launch day many people could not create accounts or finish enrolling. It became one of the most studied project failures because it was so public and so well documented.

```note
The details below are summarised from widely reported accounts. Treat figures as approximate and verify them against the primary government reviews before you quote them anywhere.
```

## What was reported

```cards
alert | Struggled from day one | Many users could not create accounts or complete enrolment, and traffic far exceeded what the system handled.
users | Split ownership | Delivery was spread across many contractors, with a government agency acting as its own integrator. Reviews pointed to unclear ownership and weak coordination.
clock | Late change, late testing | Requirements changed late and the testing window was compressed. End-to-end testing of the whole system came very late.
flag | An immovable date | A fixed political launch date was treated as immovable, while scope and readiness were not.
rocket | The recovery | A small group of senior engineers set daily priorities and tracked defects visibly. The site improved substantially within weeks.
```

## How a failure like this builds up

```flow
Fixed date | Launch date locked early
Late changes | Scope moves, testing squeezed
Unclear ownership | Nobody owns the whole
Launch | Public failure
Recovery | Visible defects, daily priorities
```

Notice that no single mistake caused it. A date that could not move, plus changes that kept coming, plus no one clearly accountable for the whole, made failure very likely before launch day.

## Symptoms versus root causes

The skill this lesson trains is separating what users saw from why it happened.

```quiz
? Which of these is a root cause rather than a symptom?
- Users could not sign up on day one
+ A fixed launch date with no clear authority to call a no-go
- The site was slow
= Symptoms are what users see. Root causes are the decisions and structures that made failure likely.
```

## The same pattern, today

The Healthcare.gov story is from 2013, but the pattern repeats in AI projects now. Imagine this message:

```quote
A competitor launched an AI assistant. We need ours live before the board meeting. | Executive sponsor
```

```cards
flag | A date that cannot move | The launch date is set before anyone knows how accurate the assistant is. | Quality is judged only after customers see it.
users | Nobody owns the whole | Data, model, app and support sit with different teams. | Each team passes the blame when it fails.
clock | Evaluation squeezed | Impressive demos are easy; careful testing of wrong answers takes time. | The first real users find the failures.
```

```ai
Lesson: AI projects repeat classic failures faster, because a demo is easy to build and proper evaluation is hard. Ask for a written go or no-go standard before the date is fixed.
```

## Questions to think about

```accordion
What were the root causes, not just the symptoms? | Symptoms are crashes and queues. Root causes sit in governance, ownership, scope control and when testing happened.
Which PM practices were weak or missing? | Look at integration management, scope control, testing, risk escalation and the go or no-go decision.
Who should have been able to say "not ready"? | Ask who had both the authority and the evidence, and why the structure let a launch go ahead anyway.
What did the recovery team do that you can reuse? | A small empowered group, daily priorities, visible defect tracking, clear decisions.
```
