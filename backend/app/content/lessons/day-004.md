## You do not need to code. You do need to understand.

Technical literacy lets you ask better questions, spot unrealistic estimates and earn the team's trust. The goal is fluency, not engineering depth.

## Four ideas to own

**SDLC (software development life cycle).** The path from idea to running software: plan, design, build, test, release, operate. Know where your project is on it and what must be true to move to the next stage.

**Environments.** Code usually moves through separate copies of the system: development, test, staging, production. Each has a different purpose. Problems found in production cost far more than problems found in test, so ask which environment a result came from.

**APIs (application programming interfaces).** A defined way for one system to ask another for data or actions. Most integrations are APIs. When someone says "we depend on the vendor's API", ask three things: who owns it, what is its availability commitment, and what happens to us if it changes.

**Cloud basics.** Instead of owning servers, you rent computing, storage and services. The provider secures the underlying platform. You remain responsible for your data, access and configuration. This is called shared responsibility.

## Questions that make you credible

- Which environment is this demo running in?
- What is the rollback plan if the release fails?
- Which other systems call this API?
- Who has production access and how is it approved?
- What is not tested yet?

## Check yourself

Write the path your project's change must take from a developer's laptop to production in your organisation. Mark each approval or gate on the path. Gaps in your answer are gaps in your plan.
