## Twenty stories by Friday

Daniel has asked for something he can see. Not a document, a live backlog he can open. Marcus has the stories drafted on paper. Rahul has a team waiting for sprint one.

```chat
# Message, Tuesday, 8:20 AM
Daniel Okafor | Sponsor | I want to open one link and see the next three sprints. Twenty stories, ordered, each one clear.
Rahul Mehta | Lead developer | Please make sure each story has acceptance criteria. Otherwise we spend planning arguing.
Marcus Webb | Business analyst | I will write them. But I need you to show me how to organise them in the tool.
```

A backlog in a tool is only as good as its structure. Twenty well formed stories, grouped under epics, ordered by value, with clear criteria, give the team a runway and the sponsor a view. Twenty vague titles in a tool give you the same confusion as before, with a nicer screen.

```key
In Jira or Azure DevOps, structure matters: epics for large outcomes, stories for slices of value, acceptance criteria in each, and an order that reflects value.
```

## The structure

```cards
layers | Epic | A large outcome, such as order tracking. Holds many stories. | An epic used as a dumping ground.
check | Story | A slice of value a team can finish in a sprint. | A task or a layer, not a user benefit.
file | Acceptance criteria | Written in the story, in Given, When, Then form. | Left blank, so testers invent them.
flag | Priority and order | Ranked by the Product Owner on value and risk. | Everything marked highest.
```

## Fields every story needs

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Title | Short, in the user's words |
| Description | As a user, I want, so that |
| Acceptance criteria | Three or fewer clear pass or fail statements |
| Estimate | Story points, set by the team |
| Epic and labels | So you can filter and report |
| Owner | Set when work starts, not before |

## Ordering by value and risk

A common mistake is to order only by value. Good Product Owners also consider risk, putting the riskiest items early so surprises appear when they are cheap.

```flow
High value, high risk | Do first, learn fast
High value, low risk | Do early, deliver value
Low value, high risk | Question whether to do it
Low value, low risk | Later, if at all
```

## Your decision

Daniel wants the backlog ordered by what looks best in a demo, with the data load last.

```decision
? What do you recommend?
- Order it as Daniel asks, since he is the sponsor | The riskiest work, the data load, is discovered in the last sprint and the date is lost.
- Ignore him and order it by technical dependency | You win the argument and lose the relationship, and the demos become dull.
+ Show Daniel a short version of the value and risk view, propose starting the data load early in a thin slice, and promise a demo every sprint using real order data | He sees visible progress and the risk is retired early.
= Order by value and risk, and keep every sprint demonstrable. Show the trade-off rather than argue.
```

## What a healthy backlog looks like

```rag
Next two sprints | G | Stories small, estimated, with acceptance criteria
Sprints three to five | A | Epics sliced, not all estimated
Later | G | Rough epics only, reviewed monthly
Hygiene | A | Fifteen stale items to close or rewrite
```

```

Use AI features to draft and to deduplicate, then read each story the way a developer will on Tuesday morning. Check the tool's data settings before enabling anything.
```

## Before you submit

```checklist
Twenty stories grouped under at least three epics
Each story follows the As a, I want, so that form
Each story has two or three acceptance criteria
Stories are sized and ordered by value and risk
At least two stories cover AI controls such as human fallback or logging
No confidential names or data
```
