## A Friday morning in July 2024

On 19 July 2024, a faulty content update to a widely used endpoint security product caused a very large number of Windows computers to crash and fail to restart normally. Airlines, hospitals, banks and broadcasters were disrupted around the world. This is one of the best known IT outages in recent years, and it is taught in project management because it was not an attack. It was a routine update.

## What reportedly happened

```timeline
Before release | A content configuration update is prepared for a security sensor | info
Release | Update is pushed to customers on Windows | warn
Within minutes | Large numbers of machines crash and enter repeated restart loops | bad
Same day | Vendor identifies the faulty update and withdraws it | ok
Days to weeks | Many affected machines need manual repair, organisations recover at different speeds | warn
After | Vendor publishes analysis and commits to changes in testing and rollout | info
```

## Why it matters to a project manager

```cards
link | Concentration risk | Many organisations depended on one product and one platform. | A single supplier failure becomes a business failure.
rocket | Release control | An update reached everyone at once, with no staged rollout reported for that content type. | One mistake with a very wide reach.
tool | Recovery needed hands | Machines that cannot boot cannot be fixed remotely in the usual way. | Recovery speed limited by people and access.
users | Dependency chains | Customers of affected companies felt the outage too. | Hidden dependencies discovered in the middle of an incident.
```



## Lessons that apply to your projects

```accordion
Stage your releases | Release to a small group first, watch, then widen. This applies to your own releases and to what vendors do to you where you can influence it.
Know your critical dependencies | List the suppliers and products that, if they fail, stop the business. Ask what their release and testing controls are.
Plan manual recovery | Know how you would recover when automated tools cannot reach a machine, including encryption keys and physical access.
Rehearse communication | Staff and customers need clear messages quickly, even when the cause is not known.
Contract for it | Check the rights you have when a supplier causes an outage, including notice, rollout controls and service credits.
```

```ref
Azure | Well-Architected Framework, reliability pillar | Staged rollouts, rollback and failure testing are reliability practices. AWS has the equivalent pillar.
ITIL | ITIL 4 change enablement and release management practices | Test updates and release in rings, and keep a rollback route.
```

## Your decision

Your company runs the same endpoint product. A colleague suggests removing it from everything, to avoid a repeat.

```decision
? What do you say?
- Agree, and remove the product everywhere immediately | You trade a known risk for a rushed, uncontrolled change and a security gap.
- Do nothing, because it was a one off | You accept the same concentration risk without any improvement.
+ Keep the control, then ask the vendor for rollout and testing commitments, enable staged or delayed update options where available, and test a manual recovery for a sample of machines | You reduce the blast radius and recovery time without weakening security.
= Do not overreact by removing security tools. Reduce the blast radius and rehearse recovery.
```

```incident
# Vendor update disabled a customer facing AI service (illustrative)
What happened | A vendor pushed an update to a component used by an organisation's AI service. The service failed and all instances needed manual repair.
Impact | Customer facing features were down until fixes were applied.
Root cause | Updates reached all systems at once, there was no staged rollout and no tested fallback for the AI feature.
Resolution | The PM added staged update rings, a degraded mode that uses a human queue, vendor dependency mapping and a recovery drill.
PM move | Map vendor dependencies, stage updates and test a degraded mode.
```

```answer
? What lessons would you take from a major vendor outage for your own projects?
- It was their fault so nothing applies | Your resilience is still your risk.
- Avoid all vendors | It is not practical.
+ I would map critical vendor dependencies, ask how updates are staged, check that recovery steps are documented and tested, define a degraded mode and communication plan, and include vendor failure in the risk register. I avoid overclaiming about causes and rely on published post incident reports. For AI features I define a human fallback.
= Dependency map.
= Staged updates.
= Tested recovery.
```
