## Two days before go-live

It is week 8 of Nimbus. Approval from Daniel is in hand. The team is ready. You are building the go-live checklist when this message arrives.

```chat
# Message, Wednesday, 4:52 PM
Elena Rossi | CISO | I was never consulted on how customer data flows into the AI vendor. This cannot go live on Friday.
Daniel Okafor | Sponsor | Elena, nobody told me this was an issue. Is this your veto or a delay?
Priya Nair | Service desk lead | And nobody asked my team how to support the assistant on day one.
```

Nobody did anything wrong on paper. You simply never mapped who could stop the project, and what each of them needed from you.

```key
Most IT projects fail because of people and decisions, not technology. Knowing who matters, and what each person needs, is the cheapest risk control you have.
```

## How the surprise built up

```timeline
Week 1 | Sponsor approves scope. Security is told "we will keep you posted" | info
Week 4 | Data flow to the AI vendor is designed. Security is not in the room | warn
Week 6 | Operations hears about the assistant from a poster in the lift | warn
Week 8 | CISO blocks go-live two days before launch | bad
Week 9 | Launch moves two weeks while the data flow is redesigned | bad
```

## The usual cast

| Stakeholder | Influence | What they need from you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsor | Funds and decides | Clear outcomes, honest status, early warning |
| CIO | Sets technology direction | Alignment with strategy and architecture |
| CISO | Can block release | Evidence of security and compliance |
| Enterprise architect | Approves design | Fit with standards, integration plans |
| Operations / service desk | Runs it after you | Runbooks, monitoring, training, handover time |
| Vendors | Deliver parts | Clear scope, timely decisions, payment on milestones |
| Business users | Adopt it | Involvement, training, a say in priorities |
| Finance / procurement | Controls spend | Accurate forecasts, contract compliance |


## Map them on two axes

Place each person by how much influence they have and how interested they are. Redo the map whenever scope or leadership changes. Elena sat in the top left, high influence and low engagement, which is where late surprises come from.

```cards
users | High influence, high interest | Manage closely. Involve them in decisions and give early warning. | They feel ambushed and turn into blockers.
eye | High influence, low interest | Keep satisfied. These are the people who surprise you late. | They appear two days before go-live with a veto.
zap | Low influence, high interest | Keep informed. They are often your adopters and advocates. | Rumours fill the silence and adoption suffers.
book | Low influence, low interest | Monitor with light updates. | Their situation changes and you do not notice.
```

```ref
PMI | Power and interest grid, a common stakeholder analysis tool in PMI material | Draw it on a whiteboard in an interview. It is quick and proves you can prioritise attention.
```


## Your decision

It is 5:10 PM. Elena has blocked the launch. The sponsor is irritated.

```decision
? What is your first move?
- Ask Daniel to overrule Elena so the date holds | You win the date and lose security's trust. If anything goes wrong with customer data, you will be named in the review.
- Apologise to everyone and wait for Elena to tell you what she needs | You look passive, the clock keeps running and Elena has to do your job.
+ Book 30 minutes with Elena tomorrow morning, ask what evidence would let her approve, and bring Daniel and Rahul so the options and dates are decided together | Elena gets a real seat, Daniel sees the trade-off between date and risk, and the team learns exactly what to build to close the gap.
= Treat a blocker as a stakeholder with an unmet need. Ask for the evidence that would satisfy them, then put the date and the risk in front of the sponsor together.
```

## New AI stakeholders

When a project includes AI, new people join the map. Add them on day one.

```cards
shield | Privacy and legal | Decide what personal data the AI may touch. | They stop the launch late over data use.
brain | AI risk owner | Someone accountable for wrong or harmful answers. | Nobody is named, so nobody acts when it goes wrong.
users | Staff who fear replacement | The people whose work the AI changes. | Quiet resistance and low adoption.
```

```ai
Shift | AI projects add new stakeholders: privacy, legal, an AI risk owner and staff who worry the tool will replace them.
Example (illustrative) | A privacy officer learns about an AI assistant from a poster in the lift. The PM adds privacy, security and an AI risk owner to the stakeholder map for every future project.
Use AI to | Draft stakeholder updates, suggest questions each group may ask and spot groups missing from your map.
Keep human | Reading the room. A message that is accurate but cold can damage trust faster than a late one.
Risk | Missing a group with power to block, such as security or privacy, until the week before launch.
Control | Update the stakeholder map each phase, and name an owner for AI risk before design starts.
Interview line | In AI projects I add security, privacy, an AI risk owner and the affected staff to the stakeholder map in week one.
```

```ref
NIST | AI RMF Govern function: roles, accountability and diverse input | New AI stakeholders include data owners, model owners, legal and risk. Name them and their accountability.
NIST | CSF 2.0 Govern function | The CISO owns cyber risk strategy under Govern. Say which security decisions you escalate to the CISO.
```

```cards
bot | Forward deployed engineer | Embedded with the customer to deploy, connect and tune the AI in their environment. Needs access, data and one named contact. | Changes agreed on site that no one approved.
shield | AI security consultant | Tests the assistant for prompt injection and data leakage and maps findings to OWASP LLM Top 10 and NIST AI RMF. | Joins in the last week, so findings force rework.
cpu | AI engineer | Builds retrieval, prompts and evaluations. Needs clean data and a measurable accuracy target. | A vague target, so done is never agreed.
wrench | Agent harness engineer | Builds the tools, memory, guardrails and logging around an agent. The term is not standardised, so confirm the scope. | Nobody owns agent permissions.
code | Traditional developers | Build the integrations, interfaces and release. Need stable requirements for the AI parts. | Treated as outside the AI conversation.
```

```incident
# Launch blocked by a missing stakeholder (illustrative)
What happened | A service desk assistant was ready to launch. In the final week the AI security consultant found that ticket text, including customer names, was sent to the vendor model.
Impact | Go-live slipped while the data flow was redesigned, and the forward deployed engineer reworked the integration.
Root cause | The stakeholder map had no AI security or privacy owner, and the data flow was never reviewed with the CISO.
Resolution | Personal data was redacted before the call, requests were logged, and written security approval was obtained before a new date was set.
PM move | Add AI security, privacy and the forward deployed engineer to the map in week one, with a review gate on data flows.
```

## Mistakes to avoid

```warn
Meeting operations only at go-live. Treating security as a final approval step instead of an early partner. Assuming the sponsor and the real decision-maker are the same person.
```

## Check your understanding

```quiz
? The CISO can block your release but rarely attends your meetings. Where do they sit on the map?
- Monitor
+ High influence, low interest: keep satisfied and bring them in early
- Keep informed
= High influence with low engagement is exactly where late surprises come from.
```

```comm
# Win over the blocker
Audience | Elena Rossi, the CISO, who has not seen the penetration test report
Situation | Go-live is two days away and she may refuse approval. You need her to engage, not escalate.
Task | Write a three sentence message that acknowledges her concern, says what you are doing about it and asks for one specific thing.
Good looks like | Opens by agreeing her sign off matters, gives a dated plan for the report, and asks a narrow question such as which findings would block approval. Respectful, factual and short.
```

```answer
? Tell me about a time a stakeholder blocked your project, or how you would handle it.
- I escalated to the sponsor so the date held | Wins by rank and ignores the real concern. Trust with security is lost.
- I waited for them to tell me what they needed | Passive. The clock keeps running and you do not own the problem.
+ Two days before go-live our CISO blocked an AI assistant over an unreviewed data flow. I met her the next morning, asked what evidence would satisfy her, and brought the sponsor and lead developer in so the date and the risk were decided together. We added redaction and logging, she approved in writing, and I now add security and privacy to the stakeholder map in week one.
= Use your own real project. Never borrow a story you cannot defend under questioning.
```

## What to remember

```checklist
Map stakeholders by influence and interest
High influence and low interest means keep satisfied, engage early
Bring operations and security in at the start, not at go-live
The sponsor is not always the real decision-maker
Treat a blocker as a stakeholder with an unmet need
```
